Software Quality Factors (McCall's Model)

Software projects usually get basic calculations right, but fail quietly on maintenance, reliability, reuse and training — because nobody wrote requirements for those aspects. McCall's model turns quality into 11 concrete, requirement-ready factors.

Why quality requirements matter

The SQA process starts at the requirement specifications stage — quality factors must be embedded from the very start. Software projects generally fulfill basic requirements for correct calculations (correct inventory figures, correct average class scores, correct loan interest), yet they routinely suffer poor performance in maintenance, reliability, reuse or training. The root cause is simple: nobody wrote requirements to cover these aspects of functionality.

McCall's model classifies the variety of issues around software attributes, use and maintenance into content groups called quality factors.

McCall's factor model

McCall's model organizes 11 software quality factors into three categories:

Product operation Product revision Product transition

Product operation factors

These five factors govern how the software behaves while it is running.

1. Correctness

Output specifications requiring correctness are multidimensional:

  • Output mission — e.g. a sales invoice printout, or a red alarm when temperature rises above 250°F.
  • Required accuracy of outputs that can be hurt by inaccurate data or calculations.
  • Completeness of the output information.
  • Up-to-dateness — the time between an event and its consideration by the system.
  • Availability — the reaction time needed to obtain requested information.
  • Coding & documentation standards the system must comply with.
Example — club membership system

Output mission: "A defined list of 11 types of reports, four types of standard letters to members and eight types of queries, displayed on the monitor on request."

Accuracy: "The probability for a non-accurate output, containing one or more mistakes, will not exceed 1%."

Completeness: "The probability of missing data about a member, attendance at club events, and payments will not exceed 1%."

Up-to-dateness: "Not more than two working days for information about participation in events."

Availability: "Reaction time for queries will be less than two seconds on average; reaction time for reports will be less than four hours."

Standards: "The software and its documentation are required to comply with the client's guidelines."

2. Reliability

Deals with failures to provide service — the maximum allowed system failure rate, for the whole system or one or more of its functions.

Example — bank system "The bank's software system will not fail, on average, more than 10 minutes per month during office hours. The probability that off-time (repair + recovery of all bank services) exceeds 30 minutes is required to be less than 0.5%."

3. Efficiency

The hardware resources needed to perform all the software's functions.

Which bid is more efficient? A chain of stores compares two bids with identical computers but different storage (250 GB/branch + 5 TB HQ vs. 200 GB/branch + 3 TB HQ) and different numbers of 10 MBPS communication lines (three vs. two per branch) — efficiency requirements must be specified precisely enough to compare such trade-offs.

4. Integrity

Deals with software system security — preventing access by unauthorized persons, and restricting "read" and "write" access.

5. Usability

The scope of staff resources needed to train a new employee and operate the system.

Example (a) A staff member should handle at least 60 service calls a day using the system. (b) Training a new employee takes no more than two days (16 hours), after which the trainee can handle 45 service calls a day.

Product revision factors

These three factors govern how easily the software can be changed after release.

1. Maintainability

The effort needed by users and maintenance staff to identify the reasons for failures, correct them, and verify the correction — supported by modular structure, internal program documentation, and the programmer's manual.

2. Flexibility

The capabilities and effort required for adaptive maintenance — adapting the software to different customers or extents of activity, and to changes in the firm's technical/commercial environment.

Example "The software should be suitable for teachers of all subjects and all school levels (elementary, junior and high schools)."

3. Testability

Special features that help the tester — predefined intermediate results, log files, and automatic diagnostics at startup.

Product transition factors

These three factors govern how the software moves across environments and projects.

1. Portability

Adapting a software system to other environments — different hardware, different operating systems, and so on.

2. Reusability

Using modules originally designed for one project in a new project — saving development resources, shortening the development period, and providing higher-quality modules (most faults were already found by QA on the original software).

3. Interoperability

Creating interfaces with other software systems or with other equipment firmware.
