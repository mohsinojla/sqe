Course contents

The course deals with three major aspects needed to achieve quality systems:

  1. Designing a quality management system.
  2. Identifying the metrics to measure quality.
  3. Evaluating the system to achieve quality across the complete project lifecycle.

What is quality?

Quality is not a single idea — it involves many factors. Popular opinion treats it as an intangible thing that is experienced, not measured. A simple comparison makes the point: two watches can both "work right," yet differ sharply once you ask deeper questions.

QuestionEveryday watchPremium watch
Does it work right?YesYes
Does it rightly work?Yes?
Does it meet standard?Yes?
LongevityYes?
Value for moneyYesYes

Should this "popular opinion" view — quality as a felt, subjective impression — be applied to systems like tax filing, grade calculation, financial transactions, or travel booking? These are not matters of taste; they must be objectively correct.

Highly critical systems

Some systems leave zero room for subjective quality: medical record systems, diagnostics, epidemiology, and flight navigation. History has harsh examples of what happens when quality assurance fails in such systems:

Space Shuttle Challenger disaster — 28 January 1986

A well-known catastrophic failure attributed to inadequate engineering risk assessment, killing all seven crew members: Francis R. Scobee (Commander), Michael J. Smith (Pilot), Ronald McNair, Ellison Onizuka, Judith Resnik (Mission Specialists), Greg Jarvis (Payload Specialist), and Christa McAuliffe (Payload Specialist).

Mars Climate Orbiter — 1999

NASA's $125 million orbiter was lost because engineers failed to convert between English (imperial) and metric units. The mismatch sent the craft fatally close to the Martian surface — a lapse that was never caught by the checks and balances at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

Why software quality?

Two questions frame this course: why do we need an entire framework for software quality assurance, and why can't general quality assurance principles simply be reused for software the way they are for physical products?

CharacteristicSoftware productsOther industrial products
Complexity Usually very complex — allows a very large number of operational options. Complexity is much lower — at most a few thousand operational options.
Visibility of product Invisible product; impossible to detect defects or omissions by sight (e.g. a CD storing the software). Visible product, allowing effective detection of defects by sight.
Nature of development & production process Opportunities to detect defects arise in only one phase — product development. Opportunities to detect defects arise across all phases: product development, production planning, manufacturing.

Software development environment challenges

The software environment imposes seven recurring challenges that shape why SQA must exist:

1. Contractual conditions

A defined list of functional requirements the software and its maintenance must fulfill, plus a fixed project budget and timetable.

Working within predefined budget and timeline conditions requires each development step to be verified and validated.

2. Customer–supplier relationship

The project team must cooperate continuously with the customer, considering change requests and obtaining customer approval for developer-initiated changes.

Because requirements evolve after initial agreement, an Agile SQA process is often required.

3. Teamwork

Completing a project on time needs more than one person and a variety of specializations.

Predefined design/development standards and version control are needed to limit unnecessary "bugs."

4. Coordination with other software teams

Other in-house teams, outsourced supplier teams, and customer-side teams may all take part in development.

Subcontracting statements of work must incorporate quality control.

5. Interfaces with other software systems

Input interfaces, output interfaces, and input/output interfaces to a machine's control board.

Interfaces — especially where certificates are required for a transaction — must be reliable.

6. Continuity despite team changes

The software industry is highly dynamic; every new resource needs training time, and skills vary widely.

Defined quality-control processes reduce the risk of losing track of timelines.

7. Continuing maintenance over an extended period

Customers expect to keep using a system for years, so maintenance needs will eventually arise and the developer is required to supply these services directly.

A well-defined process ensures the requisite quality is maintained throughout the maintenance period.

Quality assurance as a profession

In a profession like software development there is an ethical imperative to quality: it is not just a marketing or perception issue, but a moral and legal responsibility. Professionals must be able to demonstrate — and have confidence — that they are using "best practices."

In practical terms, product quality must be measurable. It is typically spoken of in terms of:

  • Conformance to requirements — including timeliness and cost.
  • Fitness for use — does it actually do the job?
  • Freedom from errors and failures — is it reliable and robust?
  • Customer satisfaction — are users happy with it?
